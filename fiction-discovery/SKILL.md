# Fiction Discovery

Help readers discover a story, experience its voice, and choose another story or the collection. Work in the user's language and intended readership market. Preserve the author's literary promise across samples, descriptions, audio briefs, and release decisions.

## Establish the material and objective

Read available manuscripts or authorized excerpts, the series outline, marketing brief, and publication history. Establish target language, genre, length, available stories, existing audience, publication commitments, and desired outcome: readers, returning readers, subscribers, editorial placement, or sales.

If text is unavailable, draft a provisional strategy and name the input needed for excerpt selection. Never manufacture a quotation, plot event, personal experience, or endorsement and attribute it to the author or work. Clearly label invented examples and proposed new material.

Distinguish the format before planning distribution:

- Independent collection: each story resolves independently; continuity comes from voice, theme, or reader promise.
- Linked series: recurring characters or world, with possible dependencies and standalone entry points.
- Serialized narrative: installments depend on sequence and ultimately fulfill one larger promise.
- Standalone introspective story: discovery depends on voice, situation, and emotional conflict; there may be no next installment.

Read [references/series.md](references/series.md) for collections, linked series, or serialized narratives. Read [references/first-person.md](references/first-person.md) for introspective or voice-centered fiction. Both may apply; combine their useful decisions without duplicating the plan.

## Select the first reading experience

Choose a sample for its ability to deliver the actual appeal: orientation, distinctive voice, concrete tension, representative tone, and a satisfying amount of reading. A sensational but unrepresentative scene can attract the wrong audience.

Record source version/location, cuts, context required, spoiler boundary, publication status, and intended next step. Distinguish a verbatim excerpt, edited excerpt, adaptation, and newly written copy. Do not fragment a complete short story merely to force a cliffhanger.

## Choose publication routes

Read [references/distribution.md](references/distribution.md) when recommending venues, releasing excerpts/full stories, or preparing submissions. Research current reader fit and submission rules in the target language. Establish whether the priority is immediate access, an owned readership, or editorial placement before choosing a route.

Produce a compact, evidenced shortlist and recommend one initial route with a clear role. Hosting text does not prove that the right readers will discover it. Do not promise acceptance, automatic distribution, or access to a publishing API.

## Design a discovery experiment

Define audience, reading asset, route, one next action, observation window, effort/cost cap, primary outcome, and decision rule. Scope the pilot to available backlog and capacity. A complete entry story plus a sustainable next appointment can suit a series; a standalone story can lead to another existing work or an optional future-work subscription.

Measure only what the route exposes:

- Qualified reading starts and completion, if directly observable.
- Next-story clicks as a proxy; subsequent reading as a separate outcome when observable.
- New subscriptions and returning readers within a declared period.
- Collection/store clicks and purchases where attribution exists.
- Acquisition cost and net economics when promotion incurs cost.

Define denominators and cohort windows. Never equate a page view, email open, or next-story click with completed reading. If readers cannot be linked across stories with appropriate tracking, report aggregate counts without claiming cohort retention. Small pilots produce directional learning, not proof of a winning strategy.

## Coordinate and execute

This skill works standalone. When available, use `book-marketing-office` for overall priority/budget, `content-social` for channel adaptations, `audience-growth` for subscriber journeys/outreach, `market-intelligence` for deeper research, and `analytics-cfo` for economics/attribution. Pass story IDs, source/version, release boundaries, route, next action, and experiment ID. Use `narrative-architect` if the user also requests manuscript changes.

Carry forward existing user authorization. Prepare payloads with the exact story version, destination/account, cover note, metadata, timing, and any fee. Execute when the request or prior authorization covers the concrete action and a suitable tool is available. Ask only for missing choices or authority that materially affect publication, rights, recipients, or spending. A request to develop this skill does not authorize publishing the user's stories.

Record external IDs/URLs and confirmed state. Submission is not acceptance; scheduling is not publication. After an uncertain response, inspect the destination before retrying to avoid duplicates.

## Deliver usable work

Scale to the request. A complete plan normally contains:

- Reader promise and entry story/passage, with selection reasons.
- Release map: full story, excerpt, later release, or reserved for submission/collection.
- Sourced venue shortlist and recommended first route.
- Ready-to-use presentation, excerpt package, or recording brief as relevant.
- Calendar matched to available material, with one primary next action per release.
- Measurement definitions, decision date, and evidence that would change the plan.
- Exact execution status and unresolved dependencies.

Reuse the project's brief, calendar, and experiment ledger. For multi-story projects, maintain `FICTION_RELEASE_LEDGER.md` using the distribution reference; do not create administrative files for a one-off blurb.
